## The core argument: own the market, cheaply
Bogle's case for **low-cost, broad-market index investing**: as a group,
investors earn the market return minus costs, so the average active investor
must underperform the index by the amount of fees and trading costs. The surest
way to capture your fair share of market returns is to **own the whole market at
minimal cost** and hold.

## Costs are the enemy of returns
Expense ratios, trading commissions, taxes and turnover compound *against* you
just as returns compound *for* you. Small annual fee differences become huge
over decades (the "tyranny of compounding costs"). Every rupee of cost is a
rupee of return surrendered with certainty.

## Most active management fails over time
Most active funds underperform a low-cost index net of fees, and the few that
win in one period rarely persist. Past outperformance is a poor predictor;
**reversion to the mean** dominates. Picking tomorrow's winning fund/manager in
advance is far harder than it looks.

## Behaviour and the return gap
Investors hurt themselves by chasing performance, trading on emotion, and timing
the market - so the *return investors actually earn* lags the *return funds
report*. Discipline, low turnover and staying invested through cycles close that
gap. Speculation is a loser's game; long-term ownership is a winner's game.

## How an SME should use it
Hold a strong **prior toward low-cost, diversified, buy-and-hold** as the default
- the bar for active, concentrated bets should be high and evidence-based. Treat
**costs, turnover and taxes** as first-order drags when sizing or recommending
trades, and be skeptical of performance-chasing and over-trading. For
index/ETF-type exposures, this is the anchoring philosophy: capture beta cheaply,
and only deviate when there is a genuine, durable edge that survives costs.
