## Low beta wins on a risk-adjusted basis
Low-beta assets deliver higher risk-adjusted returns (higher alpha and Sharpe)
than high-beta assets. The empirical Security Market Line is too flat: high-beta
is relatively overpriced and low-beta relatively underpriced versus what CAPM
predicts.

## Why: leverage constraints
Many investors want higher returns but cannot (or will not) use leverage, so they
buy high-beta assets instead. That crowding bids up high-beta prices, depressing
their risk-adjusted returns and leaving a premium for those willing to hold - and
lever - low-beta assets.

## The BAB factor
Build a factor that is long a leveraged basket of low-beta assets (scaled up to
beta 1) and short a de-leveraged basket of high-beta assets (scaled down to beta
1), so it is market-beta-neutral. This betting-against-beta factor earns
significant positive returns across equities, bonds, credit, and futures.

## Beta compression and funding risk
When funding constraints tighten (liquidity crises), betas compress toward 1 and
BAB loses money - its returns are exposed to funding-liquidity shocks. So the
premium is real but regime-dependent and can reverse sharply in a crunch.

## Practical signal
Estimate each name's beta (correlation times volatility ratio, shrunk toward 1),
then tilt toward low-beta, higher-quality names and be wary of paying up for
high-beta. The edge implicitly requires leverage on the low-beta side, so it
pairs with the sizing/leverage layer rather than naive equal weighting.

## How an SME should use it
For a given thematic view, prefer the lower-beta way to express it, and flag
high-beta crowding as a risk rather than a return source. Remember the premium
can invert in funding stress, so do not treat low-beta as unconditionally safe.
