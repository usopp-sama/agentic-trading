# Instruments primer (Family C)

## Equities
A share is fractional ownership of a business. Returns come from earnings growth,
multiple re-rating, and dividends. Single stocks carry idiosyncratic (company-
specific) risk on top of market risk. Liquidity matters: thin names have wide
spreads and gap on news.

## ETFs (exchange-traded funds)
An ETF holds a basket (an index, a sector, gold, etc.) and trades like a stock.
Pros: instant diversification, low expense ratio, intraday liquidity. Key checks:
tracking error (how closely it follows its index), expense ratio, and the
premium/discount of market price to NAV. Indian examples: NIFTYBEES (Nifty 50),
BANKBEES (bank index), GOLDBEES and SILVERBEES (commodity), LIQUIDBEES (cash
parking).

## NAV, premium and discount
An ETF's net asset value (NAV) is the per-unit value of its holdings. If market
price trades above NAV it is at a premium; below, a discount. Large persistent
gaps signal liquidity stress or stale NAV; authorised participants normally
arbitrage them away. A commodity ETF like SILVERBEES should track spot silver
(in INR) closely; a wide gap is an opportunity or a warning.

## Index / mutual funds
Pooled vehicles priced once daily at NAV. Index funds passively track a
benchmark cheaply; active funds try to beat it for a higher fee. Suited to SIPs
and long horizons, not intraday tactics.

## Sectors & themes
Group names by what drives them: financials (rate-sensitive), IT (USD/INR and US
demand), FMCG (rural/consumption, defensive), metals (global cycle), pharma
(exports + regulation), auto (rates + commodity input costs), energy (crude).
Knowing a name's sector tells you which macro pillar moves it.

## Matching instrument to thesis
A macro view is often best expressed through a broad ETF; a company-specific
edge through the single stock; a hedge through an inverse or low-beta holding.
Always weigh liquidity, cost, and how cleanly the instrument expresses the idea.
